# How should accessibility tradeoffs be communicated?

## Short Answer

Communicate accessibility tradeoffs by stating the goal, data, assumptions, alternatives, uncertainty, and people who may be affected. Explain what a modeled result supports without presenting it as a final judgment about access or priority.

## Significance

Clear tradeoff descriptions help readers understand why one route, metric, investment, or design option may favor one objective while creating a limitation for another.

## What This Means

Name the options being compared and the measure used, such as distance, slope, connectivity, coverage, cost, or redundancy. Include missing data, affected geographies, local knowledge, and the decision that remains for people or agencies.

## What This Does Not Mean

A longer route, lower score, or missing feature is not automatically a bad decision or proof of inequity. Avoid implying that a single modeled preference represents every disabled traveler or community.

## How To Use This

Use plain language, publish assumptions and source versions, invite feedback from affected communities, and validate important findings. Present uncertainty next to the result rather than hiding it in a separate note.

## Example

An agency compares a shorter route with a flatter route and explains the distance, slope, missing-data limitations, and intended user needs before asking the community which improvement should be investigated.

## Assistant Guidance

Describe tradeoffs neutrally, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and avoid ranking people or neighborhoods without a documented basis. Abstain when the objective, comparison, or affected population is unclear.
