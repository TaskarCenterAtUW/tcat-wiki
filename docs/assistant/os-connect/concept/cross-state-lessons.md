# What lessons could other states adopt?

## Short Answer

Other states can consider lessons from OS-CONNECT such as using shared pedestrian-data structures, documenting provenance and uncertainty, combining automated collection with human and community validation, and planning stewardship beyond initial data collection. Each state must adapt the approach to its laws, agencies, data, and communities.

## Significance

Statewide pedestrian-data programs require more than a technical schema. Governance, maintenance, accessibility expertise, local participation, and clear limits on interpretation determine whether the data remain useful.

## What This Means

A state can define common concepts, support interoperable formats, publish versioned releases, establish correction pathways, involve jurisdictions and disability communities, and measure coverage without treating completeness as compliance.

## What This Does Not Mean

There is no one-size-fits-all implementation, and experience from one state does not prove that a particular governance or collection model will work elsewhere.

## How To Use This

Compare the source state's context with the proposed use, document assumptions, pilot with local partners, and establish ownership and funding for updates before scaling.

## Example

A state planning a pedestrian inventory studies how shared formats and validation workflows could be adapted to its own jurisdictions, procurement rules, and community engagement practices.

## Assistant Guidance

Label these as transferable considerations rather than guarantees. Cite the documented source and distinguish observed experience from recommendations or future possibilities.
